Financial planning for business owners and the self-employed

A salaried person gets a safety net built in: EPF, gratuity, group insurance, a predictable date every month. You get none of it, and you are usually too busy running the business to build your own.

We work with founders, consultants, doctors in private practice, freelancers and family-business owners. The core job is almost always the same: separate your money from the business’s money, and build the net the business is not giving you.

The five problems we see in almost every case

Personal and business money are the same pot

The company card pays for groceries, the personal account funds a supplier. Nobody can tell whether the business is profitable or whether the household is overspending. Separating them is step one and it changes everything downstream.

No fixed salary, so no fixed savings

Income arrives in lumps, so saving happens in lumps — which means it mostly does not happen. We set a formal owner’s salary and a rule for what happens to a good month.

The business is the retirement plan

That is a single, illiquid, unlisted, undiversified asset whose value depends on you turning up. It may well be your largest asset. It should not be your only one.

No EPF, no gratuity, thin insurance

No employer is building a retirement corpus or covering your family. Term cover, health cover for the whole household and a genuine emergency fund have to be bought deliberately.

An emergency fund sized for a salary

Six months is a salaried rule of thumb. When income is variable and a client can vanish, the number is usually nine to twelve — and it has to cover both the household and the business’s fixed costs.

Tax decisions made by the accountant alone

Your CA optimises this year’s return. Nobody is optimising the next twenty years — how you pay yourself, what the business retains, and what gets moved out to safety.

What the engagement covers

  • A clean split between business and household money, with a defined owner’s salary that the household budget runs on
  • A variable-income savings rule — a base rate every month and a fixed share of every surplus month, so good quarters build something
  • Two emergency funds — one for the household, one for the business’s fixed costs, sized to how lumpy your income actually is
  • Protection — term cover, family health cover independent of the business, and a view on whether key-person or liability cover is warranted
  • Retirement that does not depend on a sale — NPS, PPF and market investments building a corpus outside the business, because a sale is a hope, not a plan
  • Concentration — how much of your net worth is the business, and a path to bringing that proportion down as you get older
  • Succession basics — nominations, a will, and what your family would actually be able to access next month if you were not here

We are not your accountant

We do not file returns, keep books, handle GST or give statutory tax opinions. Keep your CA — you need one.

What is usually missing is the person looking at your personal financial life as a whole across twenty years. That is the gap we fill, and we are happy to work alongside your accountant rather than around them.

Common questions

My income is unpredictable. Can you even plan for that?

Yes, and variability is the reason to plan rather than an obstacle to it. We work from a conservative base income for commitments, and treat everything above it as a surplus with a pre-decided split. The point is that a good month has a plan before it arrives.

Should I invest in my business or in the market?

Early on, the business usually wins — your own enterprise generally returns more than a fund. The risk is never stopping. We set the point at which money starts moving out to diversified assets regardless of how good the business looks that year.

Do you help value or sell the business?

No. That is a transaction advisory job. We plan around it: what you would need from a sale, what happens if it never comes, and how not to depend on it.
