Retirement

How much money do you need to retire in India?

Most people answer this with a round number they heard somewhere — two crore, five crore. The number is personal, and it falls out of four inputs. Here is how to work out yours, with the arithmetic shown.

  2. A worked example
  3. What your current saving builds
  4. Five mistakes that break the number

1. The four inputs

Everything else is detail. Get these four wrong and no amount of clever fund selection rescues the plan.

Your real annual expenses, today

Not your salary. What you actually spend. Then adjust for what retirement changes: EMIs that will have ended, children who will be independent, commuting that stops — against healthcare that rises, help at home you may need, and the travel people tend to do more of in the first retired decade. For most households the retired figure lands between 70% and 85% of current spending, before inflation.

Inflation between now and then

General inflation in India has averaged roughly 5–6% over long periods. At 6%, costs double in about twelve years and roughly triple in twenty. Medical inflation has historically run higher than general inflation — which matters, because that is the bill that arrives at the end.

How long the corpus must last

Retire at 58 and plan to 85 and you need twenty-seven years of income with no salary behind it. Indian life expectancy at 60 is well above the headline national figure, and it keeps improving. Planning to 85 is reasonable; planning to 90 is safer.

The withdrawal rate

What percentage of the corpus you can draw in year one, rising with inflation after that, without running out. The famous 4% figure comes from American data over American market history, and should be treated as a starting point for discussion, not a law of nature. Indian planners commonly work with 3–4% for long retirements. The lower the rate, the larger the corpus required — and the smaller the chance of an uncomfortable conversation at 78.

2. A worked example

Take a 40-year-old who wants to retire at 60 and plan to age 85.

Current monthly expenses₹1,00,000 (₹12 lakh a year)
Expenses that continue in retirement80% → ₹9.6 lakh a year in today's money
Years to retirement20
Inflation assumption6% a year
Years in retirement25

Step 1 — inflate the expense to the first year of retirement

₹9.6 lakh growing at 6% for 20 years: 9.6 × (1.06)20 = 9.6 × 3.207 = ₹30.8 lakh in your first retired year.

That number is the one people find hardest to accept, and it is simply what 6% does over two decades. The ₹1 lakh a month you spend today becomes about ₹2.57 lakh a month of the same living.

Step 2 — find the corpus that funds it

At a 3.5% first-year withdrawal rate, the corpus needed is the first-year expense divided by 0.035:

₹30.8 lakh ÷ 0.035 ≈ ₹8.8 crore.

At 4%, it is about ₹7.7 crore. At 3%, about ₹10.3 crore. The spread between those three assumptions is larger than most people's entire current portfolio, which is precisely why the assumption deserves more attention than the fund choice.

A quicker rule of thumb: at a 3.5% withdrawal rate the corpus is about 28–29 times your first-year retired expenses. At 4%, about 25 times. Multiply the inflated annual figure, not today's.

3. What your current saving actually builds

Now the other side. Suppose the same person has ₹60 lakh invested and adds ₹60,000 a month, and we assume 10% a year on a diversified long-horizon portfolio.

  • The existing ₹60 lakh compounding for 20 years: 60 × (1.10)20 ≈ ₹4.04 crore
  • ₹60,000 a month for 20 years at 10%: ≈ ₹4.56 crore
  • Total at 60: about ₹8.6 crore

Against a ₹8.8 crore target, that is close — roughly on track, with a modest shortfall and twenty years to fix it. Which is the whole point of doing the sum at 40 rather than at 55.

Note how much of that ₹8.6 crore is growth rather than contribution. Total contributed is ₹60 lakh plus ₹1.44 crore of monthly savings, about ₹2 crore. The remaining ₹6.6 crore is compounding. Time in the market is doing most of the work, which is why a decade of delay is so expensive and no later heroics replace it.

4. Five mistakes that break the number

Using today's expenses as the retirement figure

The single most common error. It makes the corpus look about a third of what it needs to be over a twenty-year horizon.

Forgetting that returns fall after you retire

A 70% equity portfolio is appropriate at 40 and usually not at 70. As the mix shifts toward debt the expected return falls, which is exactly what the withdrawal rate is trying to capture.

Ignoring the health bill

Employer health cover ends with employment, and buying comprehensive cover in your sixties is expensive — if you can get it at all with an existing condition. Personal health insurance held continuously from your forties is part of the retirement plan, not separate from it.

Counting the house

The home you live in generates no income and cannot be partially sold. It belongs on your net-worth statement, not in your retirement corpus, unless you genuinely intend to sell it and move somewhere cheaper — in which case plan that explicitly.

Assuming a straight line

Markets do not deliver 10% every year; they deliver an average with ugly stretches inside it. A bad first five years of retirement does lasting damage, because you are selling units while prices are low. The defence is two or three years of expenses held in cash and short-term debt, so you never have to sell equity in a bad year.

5. If you are starting late

Say you are 45 with ₹25 lakh invested and the same ₹8.8 crore target at 60. The existing corpus grows to about ₹1.04 crore. The remaining ₹7.7 crore needs roughly ₹1.85 lakh a month at 10% for fifteen years — which may simply not be available.

Then you change the inputs rather than pretend. Four honest levers:

  • Retire later. Five more years adds contributions and removes five years of drawdown. It is by far the most powerful lever and the least popular.
  • Spend less in retirement. Every ₹10,000 a month of today's spending you can remove takes roughly ₹88 lakh off the twenty-year target.
  • Earn something after retiring. Part-time or consulting income for even five years changes the arithmetic substantially.
  • Raise the savings rate now. Usually possible to a degree people underestimate before they see their spending written down.

What does not work is trying to make up the difference with return. Reaching for 15% where 10% is realistic is not a plan, it is a hope with a drawdown attached.
