Equity compensation

ESOP and RSU tax in India, explained simply

If part of your pay arrives as company stock, you are taxed twice on it — once as salary, once as capital gains. Understanding which event is which is the difference between a planned tax bill and an unpleasant one.

Rates and limits change with every Budget. This guide explains the mechanism, which is stable, and quotes rates as they stood when it was written in October 2026. Confirm current rates with your chartered accountant before acting.

1. ESOP vs RSU: the actual difference

Both are ways of paying you in company stock. They differ in what you have to do to get the shares.

  • ESOP (Employee Stock Option Plan) gives you the right to buy shares at a fixed exercise price. You choose whether and when to exercise. If the share price is below your exercise price, the option is worthless and you simply do not exercise.
  • RSU (Restricted Stock Unit) is a promise of actual shares, free, once a vesting condition is met. There is nothing to buy and no decision to make. An RSU has value as long as the share has any value at all.

Practically: RSUs are deferred salary paid in stock, common at large and listed companies. ESOPs are a bet on growth, common at startups, and worth nothing if the bet fails.

2. Why you are taxed twice

Indian tax treats the two things that happen to you as two separate events.

Event one — perquisite tax, at your slab rate

When the shares actually become yours, the value you received is treated as a perquisite: part of your salary income, taxed at your slab rate, with TDS normally deducted by your employer.

  • RSU: the taxable event is vesting. The perquisite is the fair market value of the shares on the vesting date.
  • ESOP: the taxable event is exercise. The perquisite is the fair market value on the exercise date minus the exercise price you paid.

Note what this means for cash flow. You owe real tax on value you have received in shares, not cash. Employers often sell a portion of the vesting shares to cover the TDS — a “sell-to-cover”. If yours does not, the tax still has to come from somewhere, and people are regularly caught out by this in a year of large vesting.

Eligible start-ups under section 80-IAC have a deferral provision for ESOP perquisite tax. It applies to a narrow set of employers; check whether yours qualifies rather than assuming.

Event two — capital gains, when you sell

When you eventually sell the shares, you pay capital gains tax on the difference between the sale price and the fair market value already taxed at vesting or exercise. That earlier value becomes your cost of acquisition, so you are not taxed twice on the same gain — only once on each slice.

If you sell immediately at vesting, the two values are nearly identical and the capital gain is close to nil. All of the tax was the perquisite.

3. A worked example

200 RSUs vest when the share price is ₹1,000. You sell them eighteen months later at ₹1,400. Your salary is already in the 30% slab.

Perquisite value at vesting200 × ₹1,000 = ₹2,00,000
Added to salary, taxed at 30% (plus cess)about ₹62,400
Cost of acquisition for capital gains₹1,000 per share
Sale proceeds200 × ₹1,400 = ₹2,80,000
Capital gain₹80,000

How that ₹80,000 is taxed depends on where the share is listed and how long you held it after vesting. For shares listed in India, held more than twelve months, it is a long-term capital gain — taxed at 12.5% at the time of writing, with the first ₹1.25 lakh of such gains in a financial year exempt. Held twelve months or less, it is short-term, taxed at 20%.

4. If your shares are listed abroad

This catches out a large number of Indian employees of multinationals, because the holding period is different.

Shares listed on a foreign exchange — a US parent company, typically — are not “listed securities” for Indian capital gains purposes. The long-term threshold is 24 months, not twelve. Sell your Nasdaq-listed RSUs eighteen months after vesting and the gain is short-term, taxed at your slab rate, which for a 30% earner is a materially worse outcome than most people expect. Held beyond 24 months, the gain is long-term at 12.5% without indexation, as the rules stood when this was written.

Two more things that apply to foreign stock:

  • Dividends are typically subject to US withholding tax (25% for Indian residents under the India–US treaty, if your broker holds a valid W-8BEN). You can usually claim that against your Indian liability as foreign tax credit, but you must file Form 67 before filing your return — miss it and the credit can be denied.
  • Currency. Your gain is computed in rupees. A flat dollar price with a weaker rupee still produces a taxable rupee gain. The reverse is also true.

5. Schedule FA: the reporting trap

If you are a resident Indian holding foreign shares — including unsold vested RSUs sitting in a US brokerage account — you must disclose them in Schedule FA (Foreign Assets) of your income tax return. This is a disclosure obligation, separate from whether you owe any tax, and it applies even if you have sold nothing and earned nothing.

It is worth taking seriously. Non-disclosure of foreign assets falls under the Black Money (Undisclosed Foreign Income and Assets) Act, which carries penalties out of all proportion to the amounts usually involved. The Income Tax Department now receives this information directly from foreign jurisdictions under automatic exchange agreements, so it is seen whether or not you report it.

Schedule FA follows the calendar year of the foreign jurisdiction rather than the Indian financial year, which trips people up. If you hold foreign stock, this is the clearest case for using a CA who handles it routinely.

6. The risk nobody mentions

Tax is the part people ask about. Concentration is the part that does the damage.

If you hold vested shares in your own employer, your salary, your bonus, your job security and a large slice of your net worth all depend on one company. If it struggles, you can lose the job and the portfolio in the same quarter. This is not theoretical — it is the ordinary experience of employees in every downturn, and the 2022–23 tech correction provided a great many Indian examples.

Two principles worth holding:

  • Decide a rule in advance, in writing. For example: sell a fixed percentage of every vest on a fixed schedule, regardless of price. A rule made calmly beats a decision made while watching the stock.
  • Set a ceiling. Many planners suggest employer stock should stay under about 10% of your investable assets. If you are far above it, bring it down over time rather than in one emotional move.

Holding on is a decision too, even when it feels like the absence of one. “I will sell when it recovers” is not a plan, and it is how a great deal of paper wealth has quietly disappeared.
