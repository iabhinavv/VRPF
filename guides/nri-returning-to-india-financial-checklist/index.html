Returning to India: the financial checklist

Moving back is an emotional decision with a long financial tail. Several of the most valuable moves are only available before you land — and most people discover them afterwards.

Rules change and residency is fact-specific. This explains the shape of the problem as at October 2026. Confirm your own position with a chartered accountant who handles cross-border cases, and with your bank, before acting.

1. Your residential status decides everything

Indian tax does not care about your passport or your visa. It cares how many days you are physically in India in a financial year, which runs 1 April to 31 March. Broadly, 182 days or more in India makes you a resident for that year; there is a second test at 60 days combined with 365 days over the preceding four years, with relaxations for Indians working abroad.

The practical consequence: the month you choose to fly home changes your tax year. Someone who returns in February may remain non-resident for that year; someone who returns in June is likely to be resident for the whole of it, with every month of foreign income already earned falling into a different regime. If your return date is flexible by a few months, the date is worth a conversation before it is worth a flight booking.

2. The RNOR window

Between being a non-resident and a full resident sits Resident but Not Ordinarily Resident (RNOR), and it is the single most valuable thing a returning NRI can understand.

You generally qualify as RNOR if you were a non-resident in nine out of the ten preceding years, or if you were in India for 729 days or less across the preceding seven years. Depending on when you return and your history abroad, this status typically lasts two to three financial years.

Why it matters: an RNOR is broadly not taxed in India on foreign income that does not arise from an Indian business or profession. Your overseas interest, dividends, rental income and capital gains generally remain outside the Indian net while the window lasts. A full resident is taxed on worldwide income.

This window is a planning opportunity with an expiry date. Decisions that are efficient during it — withdrawing from a foreign retirement account, realising gains on foreign holdings, restructuring overseas assets — may become materially more expensive afterwards. Work out exactly which financial year your RNOR status ends, and plan backwards from it.

3. Twelve to six months before you fly

  • Establish your likely residency path. Count your days. Model your return date against the financial year and find out when RNOR starts and ends for you.
  • Inventory everything. Every account, pension, brokerage, insurance policy and property in both countries, with login access, account numbers and the name of a human you can contact. Assets are far harder to deal with once you no longer have a local address or phone number.
  • Deal with foreign retirement accounts deliberately. A 401(k), IRA or workplace pension has its own rules in its own country, often with penalties for early withdrawal and treaty-dependent treatment in India. Decide whether to keep, transfer or draw down — this decision interacts directly with your RNOR window and is worth paid advice on both sides.
  • Keep a foreign bank account open if you can. Reopening one from India is difficult. Closing it costs nothing later.
  • Sort your credit life. Settle and keep documentation on foreign cards and loans. Your credit history abroad does not travel with you, and neither does theirs.
  • Plan large transfers across financial years rather than moving everything in one lump, and keep the paperwork that shows the money's source.

4. The first 90 days after you land

  • Redesignate your bank accounts. NRE and NRO accounts must be converted to resident accounts once your status changes — this is your obligation, not the bank's, and banks rarely chase it. Keeping an NRE account running as a resident is not permitted.
  • Consider an RFC account. A Resident Foreign Currency account lets a returning resident hold foreign currency in India without converting it to rupees immediately. Useful if you may go abroad again or want to avoid converting at a bad rate.
  • Check your FCNR deposits. These can generally be held to maturity even after you become resident. Breaking them early is often the more expensive option.
  • Re-paper your investments. Mutual fund folios, demat accounts and insurance policies carry your residential status. Updating KYC is tedious and gets more so if left.
  • Understand what happens to NRE interest. It is exempt from Indian tax while you are non-resident. That exemption ends with your status, and the account itself should not survive it anyway.
  • Re-examine health insurance immediately. Employer cover abroad stops. Indian health policies have waiting periods for pre-existing conditions, typically two to four years, so the sooner a policy starts the sooner it is actually useful.

5. The first two years

  • Learn Schedule FA. Once you are a resident filing an Indian return, foreign assets must be disclosed in the Foreign Assets schedule — even assets producing no income and even where no tax is due. Non-disclosure falls under the Black Money Act, whose penalties are severe, and India now receives this data directly from foreign jurisdictions.
  • Rebuild the emergency fund in rupees. Your expenses are now in rupees; your buffer should be too.
  • Re-cost every goal. Education, housing and healthcare in India inflate at different rates from the country you left, and the sums you did abroad will not hold.
  • Reassess the India property you already own. Many NRIs return to find a flat bought years ago has underperformed and costs money to hold. Owning it is a decision you can revisit, not a fact you have to accept.
  • Decide about the foreign assets you kept before the RNOR window closes, not after.
